> **Quick answer:** The AAP and NHS both advise keeping your baby in a separate cot or bassinet in your room for **at least the first 6 months**, for naps and night sleep. After that, neither sets a date. Moving is a family decision. If you move, keep every sleep safe and the routine the same, and expect a few unsettled nights.

## What the guidance actually says

**American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP).** The AAP's 2022 safe-sleep update recommends room-sharing without bed-sharing, "preferably for at least the first six months" ([AAP, 2022](https://www.aap.org/en/news-room/news-releases/aap/2022/american-academy-of-pediatrics-updates-safe-sleep-recommendations-back-is-best/)). Its parent guide says the same: share a room "for at least the first 6 months," with your baby's sleep space near your bed but not in it ([HealthyChildren.org](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/sleep/Pages/a-parents-guide-to-safe-sleep.aspx)).

The AAP's 2016 statement also said "optimally for the first year." The 2022 update no longer says that, but many websites and older articles still quote it. If you've read that you *must* wait until 12 months, that is the older wording.

**NHS (UK).** Place your baby in a separate cot or Moses basket in the same room as you, for every sleep, for at least the first 6 months ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/caring-for-a-newborn/sudden-infant-death-syndrome-sids/)). The NHS doesn't give an age after which you should move.

**Why 6 months?** These recommendations are about reducing the risk of sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS), which is highest in the early months. They aren't designed to improve sleep quality.

### What the sleep research adds

A US study of first-time mothers (the INSIGHT study) found that babies who were sleeping in their own rooms by 4 or 9 months had longer stretches of night sleep than those still room-sharing. At 9 months, early independent sleepers slept about 40 minutes more per night than room-sharers ([Paul et al., 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5495531/)). Room-sharers were also more likely to move into the parents' bed during the night.

This was an observational study. It shows an association, not that moving rooms *causes* better sleep, and it doesn't override safety guidance for the first 6 months. It is one reasonable input for families deciding what to do after 6 months.

## Is now a good time? A decision checklist

The age and safe-sleep-space checks are essential. The other items are practical considerations to help you choose a workable time, not a clinical readiness test.

- [ ] Your baby is **at least 6 months old**.
- [ ] You have a **safe sleep space** in the other room (see below).
- [ ] You can **hear or see your baby** at night from your room, by monitor or because the rooms are close.
- [ ] Night feeds are manageable from the other room, if you still give them.
- [ ] The next week is fairly calm: no illness, vaccinations, travel, starting nursery or a new sibling.
- [ ] Room-sharing is getting in the way of sleep for you or your baby (you wake each other, or your baby stirs at your noises).

If your baby is **under 6 months** and you're thinking about moving for practical reasons, talk to your health visitor or paediatrician first. The 6-month guidance is about safety, not convenience.

## Set up the new room

**Safe sleep (published guidance).** Until your baby's first birthday, the AAP advises a firm, flat sleep surface with a fitted sheet only, and no pillows, quilts, bumpers, blankets or soft toys in the cot ([HealthyChildren.org](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/sleep/Pages/a-parents-guide-to-safe-sleep.aspx)). The NHS says to keep the cot clear of pillows, duvets, loose blankets and soft toys; it permits lightweight bedding used safely or a baby sleeping bag ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/caring-for-a-newborn/sudden-infant-death-syndrome-sids/)). Always put your baby down on their back. For the cot itself, see the [bassinet-to-crib transition guide](/blog/bassinet-to-crib-transition-when-and-how-to-move-your-baby).

**Temperature (published guidance).** The NHS and The Lullaby Trust suggest a room temperature of 16–20°C ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/caring-for-a-newborn/sudden-infant-death-syndrome-sids/); [Lullaby Trust](https://www.lullabytrust.org.uk/baby-safety/safer-sleep-information/room-temperature/)). The AAP doesn't give a specific temperature. It warns that overheating increases SIDS risk and suggests one more layer than an adult would wear. Feel your baby's chest or the back of their neck. If it's hot or sweaty, remove a layer. If you cannot keep the room within that range in hot weather, focus on preventing overheating with lighter clothing and safe ventilation; check your baby regularly.

**Darkness and sound (practical suggestions).** Many families find a dark room and a steady background sound help, especially if the new room is near a street or a hallway. A research threshold should not be treated as a universal safe home volume. One study found that infant sleep machines at maximum volume could exceed the 50 dB noise limit recommended for hospital nurseries ([Hugh et al., 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24590753/)). If you use one, keep it low, place it well away from the cot, and never inside it. Our [white noise guide](/blog/white-noise-baby-sleep-benefits-and-risks) covers this in more detail.

**Monitors.** An audio or video monitor can reassure you, but it doesn't prevent SIDS. The AAP advises against using home cardiorespiratory monitors to reduce the risk of SIDS. It also says there's no evidence that consumer heart-rate or oxygen monitors, including wearables, lower that risk ([HealthyChildren.org](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/sleep/Pages/a-parents-guide-to-safe-sleep.aspx)).

For a full room checklist, see the [baby sleep environment guide](/blog/baby-sleep-environment-complete-guide).

## A one-week transition plan

*Illustrative plan. These are practical suggestions, not a clinical protocol. Adjust the pace to your baby and your home.*

| Day | What to do |
|---|---|
| 1–2 | Do one or two naps a day in the new room. Do the same short nap routine you use now. |
| 3 | First night in the new room. Do your usual bedtime routine **in the new room**, with the same sleep bag and sound. |
| 3–7 | Respond to night wakings in the same way you did before the move. Don't add a new settling method this week. |
| 7 | Review: is your baby falling asleep and waking roughly as they did before? If so, the move has settled. |

Everything about bedtime should stay the same except the room. Keep the same order, sleep bag, song and goodbye. The [bedtime routine by age guide](/blog/perfect-bedtime-routine-by-age) has examples.

**Keep timing steady too.** Moving rooms is easier when naps and bedtime are already predictable. If your baby's day has been drifting, settle on a consistent wake-up time and bedtime a week or so before the move. ## Common pitfalls

**Changing two things at once.** If you move rooms and start sleep training in the same week, you won't know which change is causing which reaction. Settle the move first.

**Responding to every noise.** Babies grunt, whimper and stir between sleep cycles. Once your baby is settled in the new room, some parents find that pausing briefly before going in lets them resettle. Always respond to genuine crying, hunger or illness.

**Judging after one night.** A hard first night is common and doesn't mean the move has failed. Review after about a week, unless your baby is unwell or very distressed. If so, it's fine to pause and try again later.

## When to talk to a professional

Speak to your health visitor, GP or paediatrician if:

- you're thinking about moving a baby **under 6 months**
- your baby is unwell, or you notice loud snoring or pauses in breathing
- your baby is still very distressed at night two or more weeks after the move
- your own sleep or mood is suffering
