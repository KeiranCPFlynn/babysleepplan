> **Quick answer:** To build a baby sleep schedule, anchor the day on your baby's usual wake-up time, add an age-typical awake stretch before each nap, and put bedtime after a slightly longer final stretch. Try it for several days, then change one time at a time. The [free schedule builder](/free-schedule) does this arithmetic from your baby's age and wake time.

## What a schedule can and can't do

A schedule is a draft of your baby's day that you test and adjust. It helps you plan when to start winding down and makes a day after a short nap easier to rescue. It can't make a baby sleep on demand, and no timetable fits every baby of the same age.

Two kinds of information go into a schedule, and it helps to keep them separate:

- **Published guidance** covers *total* sleep across 24 hours and how to make sleep safe. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine consensus, endorsed by the AAP, recommends 12–16 hours per 24 hours for infants aged 4–12 months and 11–14 hours for children aged 1–2 years, both including naps ([Paruthi et al., 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4877308/)). It did not set a range for babies under 4 months. The National Sleep Foundation's panel suggests 14–17 hours for newborns aged 0–3 months ([Hirshkowitz et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sleh.2014.12.010)).
- **Practical rules of thumb** cover nap timing and awake stretches ("wake windows"). These are widely used by parents and sleep consultants, but they are not set by the AAP, NHS or AASM. Treat them as a place to start, not a limit.

## Starting awake-stretch ranges

These ranges are a practical starting point, not clinical guidance. Many babies sit outside them and are fine. The last stretch before bedtime is usually the longest of the day.

| Age | Typical naps | Starting awake stretch (practical) |
|---|---|---|
| 0–3 months | Frequent, irregular | Short, often under 1–1.5 hours. Follow cues and feeds |
| 4–6 months | 3–4 | About 1.5–2.5 hours |
| 6–9 months | 2–3 | About 2–3.5 hours |
| 9–12 months | 2 | About 3–4 hours |
| 12–18 months | 1–2 | About 3–5 hours |
| 18–24 months | 1 | About 5–6 hours |

For more detail by age, see the [wake windows by age guide](/blog/wake-windows-by-age-the-complete-guide).

## Build a schedule in five steps

1. **Pick the anchor.** Use the time your baby actually wakes for the day on most mornings, not the time you wish they woke. A consistent wake-up time makes everything after it easier to predict.
2. **Place the first nap.** Add the low end of the starting range for your baby's age. Morning stretches are often the shortest of the day.
3. **Estimate nap length from recent days.** Use what your baby has actually been doing, not a target. If naps vary, plan with the average.
4. **Repeat for each nap.** Add the next awake stretch after each expected wake-up, usually a little longer than the one before.
5. **Place bedtime.** Put bedtime after the longest stretch of the day. Start a calm, repeatable routine 20–30 minutes before it. In a randomised trial, introducing a consistent nightly routine improved mothers' reports of how quickly young children fell asleep and how often they woke ([Mindell et al., 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2675894/)).

## Worked example: a 7-month-old on two naps

A parent's notes: wakes around 6:45 am, takes two naps of about 1–1.5 hours, bedtime has been drifting between 7 and 8 pm.

| Step | Calculation | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Wake | Anchor | 6:45 am |
| Nap 1 | 6:45 + 2.5 h awake | 9:15–10:30 am |
| Nap 2 | 10:30 + 3 h awake | 1:30–2:45 pm |
| Bedtime routine | Starts about 30 min before bed | 6:00–6:30 pm |
| Bedtime | 2:45 + 3.75 h awake | 6:30–7:00 pm |

The calculated bedtime (6:30 pm) is earlier than the family's current one. That's useful information, not a rule. They can try 6:45 pm for a week and see whether settling gets easier.

### Same baby, short-nap day

Nap 2 lasts only 35 minutes and ends at 2:05 pm. Keeping the planned 7:00 pm bedtime would mean nearly five hours awake, which is longer than this baby usually manages comfortably. Two practical options:

- Bring bedtime forward to about 6:15–6:30 pm, or
- Offer a short late-afternoon catnap (often 20–30 minutes) and keep bedtime close to normal.

## Is the schedule working? A one-week check

Run the schedule for about a week before judging it. Then use this checklist:

- **Takes a long time to fall asleep, cheerful at bedtime:** the awake stretch before that sleep may be too short. Try 15 minutes longer.
- **Fussy, rubbing eyes, or crying hard before naps:** the stretch may be too long. Try 15 minutes shorter.
- **Short naps every day despite good timing:** check the room is dark and calm, and look at how your baby falls asleep. Timing is only one factor.
- **Wakes very early every day:** look at bedtime and the last awake stretch before changing the morning.
- **Wakes in the night more than usual:** illness, teething, developmental changes and travel all play a part. Hold the schedule loosely until things settle.

Change one thing at a time, by 15–30 minutes, so you can tell what helped.

## When to rework the whole schedule

Rebuild from step 1 when:

- Your baby is consistently resisting a nap for a week or more. This can be a sign they're ready for fewer naps. See the [nap transition guide](/blog/nap-transition-guide-when-to-drop-a-nap).
- The morning wake time has moved by 30 minutes or more.
- Childcare, a new job or the clocks changing alters the day.
- Night feeds change, which can shift the morning.

## When to check with a clinician

A schedule is not a fix for a health problem. Talk to your child's doctor or health visitor if your baby snores loudly most nights, has pauses in breathing, seems unusually difficult to wake, isn't feeding or gaining weight as expected, or if you're worried about their sleep or your own wellbeing.

Whatever schedule you use, follow safe-sleep basics for every sleep, including naps: baby on their back, on a firm, flat surface, with no pillows, bumpers, blankets or soft toys. See the AAP's [guide to safe sleep](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/sleep/Pages/a-parents-guide-to-safe-sleep.aspx).
